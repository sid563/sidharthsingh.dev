Consumer groups are the part of Kafka that most people use every day and almost nobody has watched
happen. This post walks through one rebalance with a three-partition topic and two consumers written
in Rust, then adds a third consumer and watches the group reshuffle.

## The setup

One topic, three partitions, one consumer group. The producers do not matter for this post; they
keep writing order events while the group changes shape underneath them.

The consumers are the smallest thing `rdkafka` will let you write. The three settings that matter
for this post are highlighted.

```rust title="consumer.rs" {6-8}
use rdkafka::config::ClientConfig;
use rdkafka::consumer::{Consumer, StreamConsumer};

let consumer: StreamConsumer = ClientConfig::new()
    .set("bootstrap.servers", "localhost:9092")
    .set("group.id", "totals")
    .set("session.timeout.ms", "45000")
    .set("heartbeat.interval.ms", "3000")
    .set("enable.auto.commit", "false")
    .create()?;

consumer.subscribe(&["order.events"])?;
```

## Joining the group

A consumer does not talk to "the group". It talks to one broker, the **group coordinator**, chosen by
hashing the group id onto the internal `__consumer_offsets` topic.[^1] Joining is a two-round
conversation with that broker.

1. **FindCoordinator.** Any broker answers with the coordinator for `totals`.
2. **JoinGroup.** Every member sends its subscription. The coordinator waits for the whole
   generation to join, then picks one member as the **leader** and sends it everyone's subscriptions.
3. **SyncGroup.** The leader computes the assignment on the client side and sends it back through
   the coordinator, which hands each member its slice.

The leader doing the assignment, not the broker, is the detail that surprises people. The broker only
brokers. That is why assignment strategy is a *client* setting, and why two consumers with different
strategies in one group is a configuration error rather than a merge.

## Partition assignment

The strategy decides who gets what, and more importantly, how much movement happens when the group
changes. The default has moved over the years.

| Strategy | Movement on change | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `range` | High | Per-topic ranges; uneven with many topics |
| `roundrobin` | High | Even, but everything moves |
| `sticky` | Low | Keeps existing ownership where it can |
| `cooperative-sticky` | Lowest | Incremental: only moved partitions are revoked |

With the eager protocols (`range`, `roundrobin`, `sticky`) every member **revokes all partitions**
at the start of a rebalance, stops processing, and waits for the new assignment. That stop-the-world
window is the cost people mean when they say "rebalances are expensive".

<Callout type="tip" title="Use cooperative-sticky unless you have a reason not to">
With the cooperative protocol, a member keeps consuming the partitions it is going to keep. Only the
partitions that actually move are revoked, in a second, smaller round. For most services this turns a
multi-second pause into a blip on one or two partitions.
</Callout>

## Adding a third consumer

Start `consumer-c`. Its JoinGroup bumps the group's **generation**; every heartbeat from the existing
members now returns `REBALANCE_IN_PROGRESS`, and they rejoin. With three partitions and three
consumers, the sticky strategy moves exactly one partition: `p1` leaves `consumer-a`.

```text
generation 7 → 8
  consumer-a   p0 p1      →  p0
  consumer-b   p2         →  p2
  consumer-c   (none)     →  p1
```

Before `p1` is handed over, `consumer-a` gets a chance to commit its offset for it. If it does not,
`consumer-c` starts from the last committed offset, and whatever `consumer-a` had processed but not
committed is processed again. That is the at-least-once contract, and it is where idempotent
downstream writes earn their keep.

## The two timeouts that matter

Everything above is triggered by membership changes the coordinator *notices*. It notices them
through two independent clocks.

- `session.timeout.ms` is the heartbeat deadline. The consumer's background thread heartbeats every
  `heartbeat.interval.ms`; miss them for a full session timeout and the coordinator evicts the member
  and starts a rebalance. This catches crashed processes and partitioned networks.
- `max.poll.interval.ms` is the **processing** deadline. If your loop takes longer than this between
  two `poll` calls, the consumer leaves the group on its own, even though its heartbeats were fine.
  This catches a consumer that is alive but stuck.

<Callout type="warning" title="The classic outage">
A slow downstream call makes one poll loop take six minutes. `max.poll.interval.ms` is five. The
consumer leaves, the group rebalances, the partition moves to a sibling, which hits the same slow
call, which leaves too. The group spends the next hour rebalancing in a circle while lag climbs.
The fix is almost never a bigger timeout; it is bounding the work per poll.
</Callout>

## What a rebalance costs, in numbers

A rough model, for a service that processes a batch per poll:

```sql title="lag during the rebalance window"
select partition,
       max(offset_end) - max(offset_committed) as lag
from consumer_lag
where group_id = 'totals'
  and observed_at between :start and :end
group by partition
order by partition;
```

The pause is bounded by the slowest member's time to finish its in-flight batch plus one join and
one sync round trip. On a healthy cluster the round trips are tens of milliseconds; the in-flight
batch is whatever you made it. Which is the point of the whole post: **rebalance cost is mostly your
batch size, not Kafka's protocol.**

## Takeaways

- The coordinator is a broker, the assignment is computed by a client.
- Eager strategies stop the world; cooperative-sticky stops only what moves.
- Two timeouts, two failure modes: heartbeats catch dead consumers, poll interval catches stuck ones.
- Commit before you hand a partition over, and make the downstream write idempotent anyway.

[^1]: The partition of `__consumer_offsets` is `hash(group.id) % offsets.topic.num.partitions`, and the leader of that partition is the coordinator. Fifty partitions by default, so coordinators spread across the cluster.
