Part one followed a consumer group through a rebalance. This part moves to the other end of the
topic and asks a simpler-sounding question: when `send` returns, what do you know?

## acks

`acks` controls how many replicas must have the record before the broker answers.

```rust title="producer.rs"
let producer: FutureProducer = ClientConfig::new()
    .set("bootstrap.servers", "localhost:9092")
    .set("acks", "all")
    .set("enable.idempotence", "true")
    .create()?;
```

- `acks=0`: fire and forget. At-most-once, fastest, and the record may be lost before any broker
  sees it.
- `acks=1`: the partition leader has it. A leader crash before replication loses it.
- `acks=all`: every in-sync replica has it. Combined with `min.insync.replicas=2` on the topic, a
  single broker failure cannot lose an acknowledged write.

## Retries and duplicates

With retries on and idempotence off, a timed-out request that actually succeeded will be sent again
and land twice. The idempotent producer fixes exactly this: each batch carries a producer id and a
sequence number, and the broker drops a batch it has already appended.

## Where exactly-once ends

Idempotence makes a single partition write exactly-once *from the producer's point of view*.
Transactions extend that across partitions and to the consumer's offset commit. Neither reaches your
database. The moment a consumer writes to PostgreSQL and then commits its offset, you are back to
designing for at-least-once on that boundary, which is what part three is about.
